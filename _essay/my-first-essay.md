*Charlotte Kiger Price · EDPH 4070, Professor Ajua Kouadio · June 3, 2026*

## Introduction

In this paper, I will be sharing the story of how the people I come from were educated, and set this against the larger history of those institutions that did the schooling. I’ll be focusing on one main branch of my family, which is the one the most documented, and include what little I have of the rest. My family has stayed in southeastern Massachusetts for four centuries, coming over on the Mayflower. Most of the later sections of this paper will take place inside the same few square miles: the farming village of Tinkhamtown, originally part of old Rochester and now within the harbor town of Mattapoisett, nearest to the mill city (and former whaling port mentioned in *Moby Dick*) of New Bedford a few miles west. In this, I will trace how my ancestors learned, failed to learn, were kept from learning, and then eventually taught themselves, which will echo the way we have been tracing American schooling itself. We go from a single Bible in a colonial household, through the one-room district school, to the consolidated regional high school, and finally, to the research university where I now work.

## Part I. One Bible: The Colonial Roots

My family’s American story begins in 1620 with my direct ancestor Peter Brown, who sailed on the Mayflower as an unmarried man and worked as a carpenter. We know that he was literate enough to put his name to the Mayflower Compact, as one of its forty-one signers (and in a colony founded on the conviction that every soul had to read scripture for himself, the ability to sign one’s own name was important).[^1] Peter Brown came from Dorking in Surrey, marrying once he reached Plymouth. He only had daughters, so while his name did not descend from him, what descended instead was a line of women, a trade, and a book. When Peter Brown died in the 1633 epidemic that hit Plymouth, the inventory of his estate listed a single Bible, which we can assume was the formal education.[^2]

Those daughters were likely educated the way the Puritan colony generally educated its children. As Kaestle and Vinovskis describe it, early Massachusetts assumed that parents or guardians would teach children to read in order that they could read the Bible, and the colony was willing to give legal repercussions for not doing so. Peter Brown’s older daughter, Mary, was placed under the guardianship of the Assistant Governor John Doane, putting her in a likely place to have learned her letters and scripture. Around 1646, Mary married Ephraim Tinkham, which is where my family takes the name it carried for over three centuries. Ephraim’s own schooling can be seen as another type of education at the time: he had come to Plymouth at age fourteen as an indentured servant to Timothy Hatherly, and his education was an apprenticeship in labor and trade rather than in books. He rose to sergeant in the Plymouth militia and left a full legal will at his death in 1685.[^3]

Mary and Ephraim’s sons - Ephraim (jr.), Ebenezer, and Peter Tinkham among them - became early proprietors of Middleborough, at the time a frontier town that was so disrupted by King Philip’s[^4] War that it had no settled grammar school during their youth, from what I can tell. Their education would have been what their living situation demanded: being able to survey land, manage livestock, and take on the civic offices of a new town. Peter Tinkham inherited and managed the land shares that once belonged to his grandfather, Peter Brown. Peter Tinkham’s children traveled south from Middleborough into the old town of Rochester, where a cluster of Tinkhams settled the corner that came to bear their name up to the present day.[^5]

## Part II. The Tinkhams of Tinkhamtown and the District Schools of Old Rochester

That corner of Rochester, now in Mattapoisett, is still called Tinkhamtown because so many of Ephraim’s descendants lived there. The last of the original Tinkhams died in 2023, having reached 100 years of age, my great-uncle Howard Tinkham.

When I read the nineteenth-century records of my hometown, I can see my family woven into the very fabric of how children were taught at that time. Before there was any unified schoolhouse, school was held in the home of Charles Tinkham on Long Plain Road (the road I grew up on, and my parents still live on). In 1871 Thomas Tinkham sold the town a parcel of land at the elbow of Long Plain Road and Tinkham Lane so a proper district schoolhouse could be built, and when that school closed after the new village school (Center School) opened in 1899, the land passed back into the family.[^6] A 1903 map of the neighborhood shows two Tinkham sawmills, several Tinkham homes (including my childhood home), a school, a sewing circle building, and the Tinkhamtown Chapel - a small Queen Anne style building my relatives said was “only to be used for the worship of Almighty God,” on the site of a meeting house that had burned down in 1850.[^7]

The schools that my Tinkham ancestors learned in were very different from today. Under the Massachusetts law of 1789, towns were divided into districts, each governed by an elected “prudential committee-man” who hired the teacher, paid the bills, and examined the students. Rochester, adopting this system in 1799, split itself into sixteen districts, increasing to twenty-one in the 1820s as the population grew. A district might have only three months of school once in two years, and terms ran in summer and winter, children being kept home in between to work the family farm. Horace Mann later called this district-school law one of the most pernicious ever passed, because the hiring so often turned on whether a potential teacher was related to the committee-man.[^8]

As the town grew and profited from the forestry and ship-building industries, more advanced schools were opened. A private academy opened in Mattapoisett around 1856 in what was the old Congregational meeting-house, and after 1870 it became the town’s Barstow School.[^9] Rochester had built its own Greek Revival style academy in the town center in 1838 (which still stands, next to the library my mother worked in). These were the institutions that were intended to provide more than just three months of winter school for their children.[^10] When the district system was abolished in 1869, the town took over the district schools and that consolidation eventually produced the building that became my family’s educational foundation for at least four generations: the Center School in Mattapoisett, opened in 1899 as gift of the financier Henry Huttleston Rogers.[^11]

## Part III. New Bedford, the Mills, an Interrupted Education

The other root of my family is New Bedford, the industrial city nearby. When the whaling industry collapsed after the Civil War, cotton textiles became one of the main economic industries of New Bedford. By the early 20th century, it was one of the two great centers of American cotton textile production, with one of the highest shares of its workforce in industrial labor of any city in the country at the time. Schooling in New Bedford sorted children by social class. Children worked in the mills and the city ran special “mill schools,” over thirteen-week sessions so that the students could meet the legal minimum. School officials argued over whether mill children should be allowed to mix with the “primary” children of better families, and the curriculum emphasized manual training - sewing, drawing, and eventually woodworking - to fit working-class youth for factory work. One prominent resident summed up the result of this class system in 1896, saying there were “virtually three New Bedfords,” and the children of the mill districts rarely got beyond grammar school.[^12]
{% include feature/image.html objectid="louis_birth_record" %}

It was into this version of New Bedford that my maternal great-aunt Ruthie ended her formal schooling in the eighth grade to work in the mills, helping her younger brother - my maternal grandfather Louis - stay in school. In honor of her sacrifice, I carry her middle name as my first name. Her education was the cost of his, exemplifying the class-sorting the historians describe. Coming from Quebecois immigrant parents, my great-grandfather, a factory worker making eyelets, died when Louis was four years old, leaving a young wife, Annie (Marshall) Roberts to care for both children. From the 1930 census, I can see that Annie could read and write, but never attended the formal district schools, and that Ruthie (then age 8) was attending school. The 1940 census was answered by Ruthie, then 18, who reported that her mother Annie (then 40) had never completed school past the fifth grade.

Louis went to the newer New Bedford Vocational school - “the Voke” as it’s still called - and, as I’m told, lied about his age to enlist in the Army for World War II. After the war, he used the GI Bill to earn an associate’s as the New Bedford Textile Institute, an institution later folded into Southeastern Massachusetts Technological Institute (now UMass Dartmouth), where he later taught engineering. My grandmother’s younger brother (my great-uncle Howard) earned a bachelor’s degree and taught there briefly as well. In a single generation, my family moved from the mill floor to the engineering classroom of the same institution.

My father’s side of the family is rooted in New Bedford, as well. My paternal grandfather, Oman Price, graduated from New Bedford High School in 1935 and later took extension courses at the Voke. He and my grandmother Irene both earned real-estate licenses through the Lee Institute in 1963, a reminder that for their generation, education often continued as practical, credentialing courses taken well into adulthood.

## Part IV. Walking to the Herring Weir: Consolidation

By the time my great-grandparents Minnie Gamans and her later husband Earl Tinkham came along, Mattapoisett had consolidated its small single-room schools into the Center School, and both of them attended it. Minnie went on to become the cook there, leaving the job sometime before 1960, so that when my mother Gail began Center School as a first-grader (Kindergarten was not yet an option in the town), she just missed finding her own grandmother in the kitchen. Some of Minnie’s school recipes are still in my keeping. Minnie also organized, for decades, the annual Christmas carol sing at the Tinkhamtown Chapel which still occurs today.

My grandmother Marion was Minnie and Earl’s daughter, a Tinkham of Tinkahmtown. She only had two siblings, younger twins named Howard and Ethel. Marion also attended Center School through the ninth grade, and then went to high school in the larger neighboring town of Fairhaven, as Mattapoisett had none of its own. To get there, she walked roughly three miles “to the herring weird” in order to catch the school bus. She graduated in 1938 and went on to Kinyon-Campbell, a business and secretarial school in New Bedford, which was a practical and respectable result of education for a young woman from a farm at that time and where my paternal grandmother Irene also attended. Marion’s marriage to Louis joined the story of district schools with vocational schools.

## Part V. Old Rochester Regional: My Parents’ Generation

Until the late 1950s, ninth-graders in the three towns that made up old Rochester were still bused out to Wareham, Fairhaven, or New Bedford for high school. The towns finally built a shared high school, with Old Rochester Regional (ORR) opening its doors in 1961. My family was there at the beginning, with my father’s older sister Linda being in the school’s first graduating class, and Ethel’s children Ann and Alan following a couple of years later.

After graduating from ORR, my mother began college at Bridgewater State College (now University), but after her father died, she transferred to Southeastern Massachusetts University, where she studied under Howard Glasser who was a central figure in the regional folk-music and dance revival (and who taught at Carnegie Mellon before SMU!).

Her younger brother Mark’s education went quite differently. Almost certainly left-handed, he was forced to write with his right hand and struggled in school, nearly flunking out. He only graduated high school thanks to the short-lived alternative school Bent Twig that began in the basement of Center School. There he learned to play the pennywhistle as well as carpentry (and also where he dispatched a school toilet with a cherry bomb). After high school, he left for Ireland to play folk music. It was an education, of a kind, and also a reminder that the institutions often failed the people they were meant to serve.

My father started at Center School, but like my Uncle Mark, did not thrive there, and was sent to a private Christian school in the nearby mill city of Fall River before returning to high school at ORR. He earned an associate’s degree at Bristol Community College, taking some courses at SMU after. Then, decades later, at the age of fifty, he completed a bachelor’s degree at Northeastern University, summa cum laude, graduating at the same time as I did.

## Part VI. Librarians

Unlike my mother and the rest of the family, my education started early with preschool, having two working parents. For elementary school, I joined the rest of my family (cousins and ancestors) at Center School. After my public school failed to adequately deal with a bullying situation, I transferred to a small Quaker school for middle school, with less than thirty students in my grade. But like everyone else, I was back in ORR for high school, a much larger world with over one hundred students in my class. I did well in school (except for math) and attended Sarah Lawrence College on scholarship.
{% include feature/image.html objectid="louis_reading_with_daughter" %}

I went immediately from Sarah Lawrence to Drexel University for a master’s in library and information science, which I finished in March 2010 - the same degree, from the same school, that my mother had earned roughly thirty years before, though she became a public librarian while I went the academic route. In 2019, I began my doctorate in education at Teachers College, Columbia University, which I am close to completing. Academic librarians need two masters degrees, or a doctorate, and my employer Barnard College would only pay for a degree higher than any you already held. Since 2022, I have been a faculty librarian at Carnegie Mellon University, where I now teach (like my grandfather and great-uncle). The next turn has already begun: my daughter Dorothea, turning three this month, attends Carnegie Mellon’s Cyert Center for Early Education, itself a small piece of educational history, founded in 1971 by a group of women on the faculty who needed full-day childcare, begun with only twelve children in the basement of a dorm.[^13]

## Part VII. The Voices We Do Not Hear

This paper leans on branch because that is the branch the records favor. Most of the Tinkham line is easily accessible to me - through Mayflower genealogies, colonial records, and town histories, not to mention the fact that English-speaking Protestant landholders were the people early American institutions were built to document. My other ancestors who arrived later left fainter trails. The Welsh, Swedish, and French-Canadian people I also descend from reached southeastern Massachusetts in the age of the mills, not the meetinghouse.

The deepest silence to me is older than any of them. The few square miles I keep returning to were not empty when Peter Brown’s descendants moved into them, they were owned by the Wampanoag. Rochester was incorporated in 1679 on ground the colonists recorded by its Wampanoag name, Sippican; Mattapoisett is itself a Wampanoag word usually translated as “place of rest”; and the harbor had been “purchased” from the Wampanoag sachem Metacomet by William Brenton in 1664. The educational story I have been telling opens with that single Bible in 1620, but the land already held people with their own ways of teaching and remembering, and the opening that let English families settle Tinkhamtown was forced by King Philip’s War, breaking Wampanoag power across the region. The same Puritan conviction that placed a Bible in Peter Brown’s estate and encouraged literacy also produced, in 1663, the Eliot Indian Bible, as the Puritans translated their scripture into Wampanoag to create an instrument of conversion and schooling. The drive to make everyone literate in English scripture, which in my family looks like inheritance, is an erasure from the other side.

That erasure was not wholly complete, and the Wampanoag people are still here. Since 1993, the Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project, founded by Mashpee linguist Jessie Little Doe Baird, has worked to bring back a language that had gone six generations without a speaker. A people the schools were built to convert and dissolve have used the archive to teach their own children their first words again. I cannot trace the particular Wampanoag community whose ground became Tinkhamtown with anything close to what I can for my own family. Whose learning gets written down and whose gets written over is in itself a large part of the history of American education and my own education.
